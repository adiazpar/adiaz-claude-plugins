# Timing: the "why now" behind a direction

This is the layer that reads the *moment* a direction sits in — what changed recently that makes it more (or less) winnable today than a year ago. It is the one thing a co-founder with live web access brings that a founder sitting alone can't: a dated, sourced read on the window.

**Timing is evidence that feeds the four lenses — it is not a fifth lens.** You do not score it 1–5. You use it to *sharpen* the four scores in `lenses.md` with fresh, dateable signal. Read this file together with `lenses.md` before the Stage 2 survey.

## The two guardrails (load-bearing — read first)

Folding "latest news" into idea discovery walks straight at the trap this whole tool guards against: chasing what's hot. Two rules keep it honest.

1. **Timing never originates a direction, and never overrides the lenses.** Directions still come from the founder's genuine interest and skills (Stage 1) and still have to clear reachability, shippability, money-moves-here, and durable interest. A hot window for something the founder doesn't care about and can't reach is not a direction — it's a headline. Timing only sharpens directions that already belong on the table.
2. **The absence of a "why now" is not a strike.** Plenty of durable, profitable businesses have no special timing — they solve an evergreen problem for a reachable customer. A missing timing signal *never lowers* a direction. A strong one *can raise* confidence. Timing is upside-only evidence: it can promote, it cannot demote. Never penalize a sound evergreen direction for failing to be trendy.

If you ever catch yourself recommending a direction *because* it's in the news, stop — that's the trap. The news earns its place only by moving one of the four lenses for a direction the founder would want anyway.

## Durable shift vs. fad

Not all timing is equal. Separate a structural change from a passing spike, out loud, for every "why now" you raise.

- **Durable shifts** — these move the lenses for real:
  - **Regulation** — a new or changed law creates a compliance need someone must now pay to meet.
  - **Cost-curve drop** — a capability got dramatically cheaper (an API price cut, a model that makes a previously-unbuildable feature a weekend's work), so a payable v0 is suddenly in reach.
  - **Platform shift** — a new API, a deprecation, a marketplace opening or closing, a migration wave that newly clusters an audience.
  - **Incumbent stumble** — a price hike, a prolonged outage, a pivot or shutdown, an acquisition that orphans a user base now looking for an exit.
  - **Durable behavior shift** — a change in how people work or buy that has staying power, not a meme.
- **Fads** — a viral moment, a hype-cycle peak, a seasonal bump. A fad is a reason to move *fast* **only if the other lenses already hold**; on its own it is never a reason to build, because by the time a v0 ships the window may be gone.

The test mirrors the durable-interest lens: **"will this still be true in 12 months?"** If yes, it's a durable shift and you can lean on it. If no, name it as a fad and treat it accordingly.

## How a timing read feeds each lens

A "why now" earns its keep by moving a specific lens. Name which one(s), and how:

- **Money-moves-here** — a new regulation, an incumbent price hike, or fresh funding flowing into a space is direct evidence cash is moving (or is about to). The strongest, most common way timing helps.
- **Reachability** — a platform shift or an orphaned user base can newly *cluster* a reachable audience: people migrating off a shut-down tool, all complaining in the same forum this month, are far easier to reach than a diffuse market.
- **Learnability / shippability** — a cost-curve drop can turn a direction that needed months of capability into one a founder can ship a payable v0 of in weeks.
- **Durable interest** — timing does **not** feed this lens. Interest is the founder's, never the market's. Do not let a hot window masquerade as the founder caring; that confusion is exactly how founders end up a year into something they never wanted.

## Recency and sourcing

The evidence discipline from `facilitation.md` applies in full — timing is no exception, and stale or guessed timing is worse than none.

- **Date-stamp every timing claim against today's date.** A "why now" with no date is a vibe. State when the signal happened and how fresh it is.
- **Prefer primary sources** — the regulation text, the pricing page, the changelog, the shutdown notice — over second-hand commentary about them.
- **Cite it or label it inference**, same as any other market claim. "The evidence shows X (dated, sourced); my read — not sourced — is Y."
- Weight recent sources, but don't mistake recency for importance: an old structural shift still compounding beats a fresh headline that changes nothing.

## Writing the timing read

The portable output, used in the Stage 3 shortlist and carried into the brief: a **1–2 sentence "why now"** that states the **dated event**, **which lens(es) it moves**, and whether it's a **durable shift or a fad**. When a direction has no real timing signal, say so plainly — "no strong timing signal, and that's not a mark against it." This read is what `/cofounder resume` re-checks later to ask whether the window still holds.
